Choosing a Wallet

What a Bitcoin wallet really is, and how to pick one

Once you've decided to own your keys, you need a wallet. But "wallet" is a slightly misleading word, so before you pick one, it helps to understand what a Bitcoin wallet actually does.

1. A Wallet Holds Keys, Not Coins

Your bitcoin never leaves the blockchain, the shared global ledger we covered in Bitcoin 101. A wallet doesn't store coins; it manages your keys: the secret that proves the coins are yours. It does three jobs:

  • Creates addresses so people can send you bitcoin.
  • Guards the private keys needed to spend it.
  • Builds and broadcasts your transactions to the network.

So the wallet is really a keychain. Control the keys and you control the coins; lose the keys and you lose access. That's why your recovery phrase, the list of words your wallet gives you when you set it up, matters more than the app itself.

2. Custodial vs. Self-Custody

A custodial wallet (like leaving bitcoin on an exchange) means someone else holds your keys. It's really an IOU: convenient, but freezable, hackable, and only as trustworthy as the company. A self-custody wallet means you hold the keys. As the saying goes, not your keys, not your coins. Everything below is self-custody. If you want the deeper dive on doing it safely, including recovery and inheritance, see our guide to owning your keys.

3. Hot vs. Cold

The other big distinction is where your keys live:

  • A hot wallet is software on an internet-connected phone or computer. Convenient and quick, great for spending and smaller amounts, but the keys sit on a device that's online.
  • A cold wallet is a dedicated signing device that keeps your keys offline. It's the gold standard for savings you intend to hold.

Match the tool to the amount: keep pocket money in a hot wallet and serious savings in cold storage, the same way you carry some cash in your pocket but keep the rest somewhere safer.

4. Which Wallet Should You Use?

There are many good Bitcoin-only wallets. Here are dependable, open-source picks for each job.

New here? Start free. You do not need to spend money on cold storage to begin. A good software wallet costs nothing and is the best place to learn: receive a little, send a little, back it up, restore it. Build your security in step with your skills, and add a signing device once your savings (and your confidence) have grown enough to deserve one.

Signing devices: for cold storage

You will see these sold as "hardware wallets," but the name is off. The coins never leave the Bitcoin network; the device keeps your keys offline and signs transactions, which is why Bitcoiners call them signing devices. All three of our picks are ones you build yourself, and that is deliberate: generic parts mean there is nothing to tamper with in the mail, and the build teaches you exactly what is guarding your savings.

SeedSigner

An open-source, air-gapped signer you build yourself from inexpensive parts. It keeps your keys completely offline and signs entirely by QR code.

Read our SeedSigner guide →

Krux

Open-source firmware that turns cheap, off-the-shelf touchscreen devices into an air-gapped signer. Everything moves by QR code, multisig included, and a build costs well under $100.

Read our Krux guide →

Specter DIY

A build-it-yourself, air-gapped signer that talks entirely by QR code. It also supports Miniscript, which makes it a natural partner for Liana's recovery paths.

Read our Specter DIY guide →

The strongest setup pairs a software wallet with a signing device: the software (Sparrow, Electrum, or Cove) is the interface that watches your balance and builds transactions, while the signer (SeedSigner, Krux, or Specter DIY) holds your keys and signs air-gapped, by QR code or microSD, never over a cable to your computer.

5. Why Bitcoin-Only?

You'll notice every wallet above is Bitcoin-only. That's deliberate. Many popular wallets, including hardware devices like Trezor and Ledger, also support hundreds or thousands of other coins and tokens. That sounds like a feature, but for your security it's a liability.

More coins means a bigger attack surface. Supporting every altcoin and "Web3" network takes far more code and complexity, and that creates more ways for things to go wrong:

  • Blind signing. Spending on smart-contract networks often means approving transactions your device can't fully show you. It's one of the most common ways people get drained. Bitcoin's simpler design lets your device show you exactly what you're approving.
  • More code, more bugs. A Bitcoin-only wallet does one job, so its software is smaller and easier to audit. Every extra coin is extra code that can hide a flaw.
  • No nudging into altcoins. Multi-coin wallets are built to upsell speculative tokens and "earn" schemes. A Bitcoin-only wallet keeps you focused on sound money.

The track record matters too. Ledger suffered a major customer-data breach in 2020 that exposed hundreds of thousands of customers to phishing and worse; in 2023 its "Recover" service revealed that the device's firmware could extract and split up your seed, undercutting the promise that keys never leave the device, and a compromise of Ledger's own software library drained funds from people using it. Trezor supports thousands of tokens, and security researchers have demonstrated physical seed-extraction on models built without a dedicated secure element.

The point isn't that those companies are evil. It's that a general-purpose, do-everything wallet is the wrong tool for protecting serious savings. Bitcoin-only signers like SeedSigner do less on purpose, and that focus is exactly what makes them safer. If a wallet is trying to be a casino for a thousand coins, it isn't built to be a vault for your Bitcoin.

6. A Sensible Path

You don't have to do everything at once. A good path for most people:

  • Start small and free. A software wallet costs nothing. Put a little bitcoin in one and learn the ropes: receiving, sending, and backing up your recovery phrase.
  • Grow into cold storage. As your savings grow, move them to a signing device, ideally one you built yourself.
  • Back up properly. Write your recovery words on paper, then metal. Never a photo, never the cloud, and never share them with anyone.

Stay safe. No legitimate wallet or person will ever ask for your recovery phrase. Limestone City Bitcoin will never DM you first or ask for your seed words. Anyone who does is a scammer.
