The Bitcoin Network

Nodes, miners, and how the network agrees — without a boss

Bitcoin 101 explained why Bitcoin matters. This guide is about how it actually runs. There's no Bitcoin company, no head office, and no master server — just thousands of independent computers around the world, following the same rules and agreeing on the same ledger. Here's how that works.

Nodes: The Backbone

A node is simply a computer running Bitcoin software. A full node does three jobs that make the whole system tick:

  • Stores the blockchain — its own complete copy of every transaction ever made.
  • Validates everything — it checks every transaction and every block against Bitcoin's consensus rules (valid signatures, no double-spends, the right block reward, the 21-million cap, and more).
  • Relays — it passes valid data on to other nodes.

Crucially, every node is equal, and each one verifies for itself. Nobody is in charge. This is where the phrase rules without rulers becomes concrete: the rules are enforced by everyone, not by anyone.

A Peer-to-Peer Gossip Network

Nodes don't connect to a central hub. Each one connects to a handful of peers, and information spreads by gossip: you tell your peers, they tell their peers, and within a few seconds a message has flooded across the entire planet. There's no broadcaster to shut down and no switchboard to tap — the network has no centre.

The Life of a Transaction

When you send bitcoin, your wallet broadcasts the transaction to a few nodes. Each node that receives it validates it — are the signatures valid? are the coins real and unspent? — and, if it checks out, holds it in its mempool (the pool of pending transactions) and relays it onward. If a transaction is invalid, honest nodes simply drop it and refuse to pass it on. The network polices itself at every hop.

Mining: Turning Energy Into Order

Pending transactions need to be put in a permanent order that everyone agrees on. That's the job of miners. A miner gathers transactions from the mempool into a candidate block, then competes to find a valid proof-of-work:

  • It repeatedly runs the block's header through a hash function, changing a number called the nonce each time, searching for a result below a network-wide target.
  • There's no shortcut — it's pure trial and error, trillions upon trillions of guesses per second. The lower the target (the higher the difficulty), the harder the puzzle.
  • On average, somewhere in the world a miner wins this lottery about every 10 minutes. Every 2,016 blocks (roughly two weeks) the difficulty automatically re-tunes to keep that 10-minute pace as mining power comes and goes.

Why would anyone spend all that electricity? The winner earns newly created bitcoin (the block subsidy) plus the fees of the transactions they included. And that very real energy cost is the point: it's what makes rewriting history prohibitively expensive.

Blocks and Validation

When a miner finds a valid block, it gossips out across the network just like a transaction. Then every node independently checks it: is the proof-of-work valid? are all the transactions legitimate? is the reward correct? does it build on the previous block? Only if everything passes does a node add the block to its chain and start building on top.

This is the part people miss: miners propose, but nodes decide. A miner who tries to sneak in a fake transaction or print extra coins simply has their block rejected by every honest node — and forfeits all that electricity for nothing.

Consensus: The Most-Work Chain

With thousands of independent nodes, how does everyone agree on one history? They all follow a single tie-breaking rule: the valid chain with the most cumulative proof-of-work wins. Each block cryptographically points back to the one before it, forming a chain; the "heaviest" chain — the one the most energy was spent building — is the truth. To rewrite it, you'd have to out-mine the entire honest network.

When Two Blocks Collide: Reorgs

Occasionally two miners find a block at almost the same moment, and the network briefly splits — some nodes see one, some see the other. The tie breaks at the next block: nodes switch to whichever branch now has more work, and the transactions from the abandoned (orphan) block return to the mempool to be mined again. This is called a reorganization, and it's exactly why waiting a confirmation or two adds safety — see Using Bitcoin as Money.

Why It Matters: No Gatekeepers

Anyone with a computer and an internet connection can join — no application, no permission. That means no one can stop your transaction from spreading, and no one can quietly change the rules. To censor or rewrite Bitcoin, an attacker would need to out-compute the whole honest network at once, which is astronomically expensive. The result is censorship resistance: money that works the same for everyone, everywhere.

Run Your Own Node

You don't have to take anyone's word for any of this. Running your own full node is the purest form of don't trust, verify: it enforces every rule for you, checks every payment you receive against the real consensus rules, frees you from relying on third parties, and adds one more independent guardian to the network. It's also more accessible than it sounds — modest hardware and a download is all it takes, and you can point a wallet like Sparrow straight at it.
